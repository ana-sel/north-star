# Plan Tab — Compound Effect and Energy Management

> Design reference spec for the Compass **Plan** tab.  
> Sources: *What Successful People Do Every Day — The Compound Effect (Superposition ep. 7)* · *How to Get Everything Done: An Immediately Usable Power-Management Method*

---

## 1. Core Teachings (distilled)

### Philosophy in 5 bullets

- **The compound effect is the most powerful force in human development — and the most underused.** Small, regular actions build on each other exponentially. The results are invisible at first, then suddenly massive. Most people quit during the invisible phase, never reaching the inflection point.
- **The actual constraint is almost never time — it is energy.** Every person has 24 hours. The practical difference between days of high output and low output is rarely the number of hours available: it is the quality of attention and energy within those hours.
- **Recovery is not a reward after collapse — it is a prerequisite for performance.** The transcript's central rule: never reach the "7% battery" state. Recovery must be planned before depletion, not after.
- **Two hours are not equal.** Two hours of clear, energized focus are worth 10 times two hours of exhausted, distracted time. A calendar filled with hours means nothing without knowing the energy quality of those hours.
- **Sustainable effectiveness requires matching task type to energy type.** Not all work is the same. Deep intellectual work, emotional conversations, physical effort, and administrative tasks each draw from different energy reservoirs. Scheduling without energy-type matching is a recipe for exhaustion and mediocre output.

### Key metaphors and mental models

| Metaphor | What it means |
|---|---|
| **The snowball** | A tiny snowball pushed from the top of a mountain barely grows at first. With each revolution it picks up more surface area, which picks up more snow, which increases surface area further. Most of the growth happens near the bottom — after most of the distance has been traveled. Compound results are back-loaded. |
| **Warren Buffett's 90% after 56** | Buffett started investing at 11 and earned 90% of his entire wealth *after* age 56. The compound curve is almost flat for decades, then nearly vertical. Quitting early means quitting in the flat part, never reaching the curve. |
| **The battery scale (0–100)** | Personal energy as a battery percentage. The aim is never to run it below 20. Recovery should begin at 40, not 0. Most people run at 7% then wonder why their days feel chaotic. |
| **Energy types (4 quadrants)** | Physical (body, sleep, movement), Emotional (interest, joy, relationship quality), Intellectual (focus, clarity, complex decisions), Spiritual (meaning, mission connection, sense of right direction). Each is a separate reservoir; depleting one does not automatically restore the others. |
| **Activity before/after table** | Track your battery level before and after each activity for one week. The pattern reveals your personal energy sources and drains with more precision than any generic advice. |

## 2. The Framework / Method (step-by-step)

### The Compound Effect: how to activate it in goal planning

**What the compound effect requires:**

1. **Consistency over intensity** — small, regular, repeated actions compound. Occasional heroic bursts do not.
2. **The right base action** — compound applies when each action builds on the last. If actions are unrelated or reset each time, there is no compound; there is just repetition.
3. **Patience through the invisible phase** — the first weeks or months of compound effort are nearly invisible. This is where most people quit.
4. **Measurement of the right thing** — you must track the *base action* (the thing being compounded) not just the downstream outcome (which lags by design).

**Compound effect in personal planning:**

| Domain | Base action (compound unit) | What it compounds into |
|---|---|---|
| Skill development | 30 min daily deliberate practice | Mastery — the curve accelerates after ~100 hours |
| Relationships | One genuine, meaningful interaction per week | Trust and depth — compounds over months and years |
| Financial | Consistent savings/investment before spending | Wealth — the classic interest compounding curve |
| Knowledge | One focused learning block per day | Expertise — accumulated models that make new learning faster |
| Health | Daily movement, consistent sleep timing | Baseline energy and resilience — most visible after 3–6 months |
| Mission work | Weekly reflection on direction | Clarity — gradually eliminates noise, surfaces signal |

**The invisible phase and how to survive it:**

The compound curve has three zones:
1. **Flat (months 1–3):** Almost no visible results. This is the danger zone — the most common quitting point.
2. **Slope (months 4–12):** Results begin to appear. Confidence builds. The action becomes a habit.
3. **Steep (12+ months):** Results accelerate. The base action now compounds on a larger base. Each next unit produces more than the last.

*Design implication:* The app must support users *through* the flat phase. This means celebrating the input (consistency of base action), not the output (which hasn't arrived yet).

### Energy Management: the full method

#### Step 1: Add an energy label to every calendar block

- **H (High):** Strategy, writing, difficult decisions, learning, creative work
- **M (Medium):** Standard meetings, analysis, professional communication
- **L (Low):** Simple administration, routine calls, inbox management
- **R (Recovery):** Sleep, walking, silence, family time, enjoyable activity

Rule: Never place two H-blocks back to back without an R between them.

#### Step 2: Track the battery scale daily (7 readings for 7 days)

Track personal energy level (0–100) at five points each day:
- After waking
- Before main work block
- After lunch
- End of work
- Before sleep

After 7 days, patterns emerge:
- Natural peak hours
- Predictable crashes
- Which activities reliably raise or lower the battery

#### Step 3: Build the personal energy map

| Activity | Before (0–100) | After (0–100) | Difference | Category |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deep work | 70 | 55 | –15 | Drains but valuable |
| Walk outside | 45 | 70 | +25 | Replenishes strongly |
| Long meetings | 60 | 30 | –30 | Drains without sufficient value |
| Learning | 65 | 75 | +10 | Replenishes slightly |

Categories:
- Replenishes strongly → Do more of this
- Replenishes slightly → Include regularly
- Neutral → Acceptable
- Drains but valuable → Surround with recovery
- Drains without sufficient value → Reduce or eliminate

#### Step 4: Match task type to energy type

| Task type | Best energy state | Worst energy state |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy / complex decisions | High intellectual, emotionally calm | Exhausted, emotionally disrupted |
| Creative writing / design | High intellectual + high emotional | Flat, distracted |
| Difficult conversations | Medium-high emotional, physically rested | Low physical, already emotionally depleted |
| Administrative / inbox | Low energy is acceptable | High energy is wasted here |
| Learning / study | High intellectual | After long emotional drain |
| Physical training | Medium physical | Below 30% physical battery |

#### Step 5: Energy budget for the day

Treat morning energy as 100 units. Estimate the cost of each planned block:

- Deep work / strategy: 25–40 units
- Important meeting: 15–25 units
- Difficult conversation: 20–30 units
- Routine administration: 5–10 units
- Recovery activity: +10–30 units (replenishment)

Rule: Plan only 60–70% of the expected daily capacity. The remaining 30–40% is a buffer for the unpredictable demands that arrive in every real day.

If your planned blocks sum to more than 70% of capacity, remove or delegate the lowest-leverage items.

#### Step 6: The recovery menu (pre-decided, not improvised)

Recovery decisions made in advance are better than decisions made at the moment of depletion (when decision-making is already compromised).

**5-minute recovery:**
- Breathing (slow, deliberate)
- Water
- Step outside briefly
- Eyes closed, no screen
- Simple stretch

**20-minute recovery:**
- Short walk
- Short nap (if timing is right)
- Quiet meal with no screens
- Shower
- Easy, enjoyable conversation

**1–3 hour recovery:**
- Nature
- Exercise
- Hobby
- Family activity
- Complete information break

*Match recovery type to depletion type:*
- Social exhaustion → solitude, nature, silence
- Intellectual exhaustion → physical movement, something simple and pleasant
- Emotional exhaustion → safety, warmth, no demands
- Physical exhaustion → sleep, food, gentle movement

#### Step 7: Fix leaks before adding replenishment

Margulan's caution: you cannot fill a leaking tank. Before adding recovery practices, identify and close the major energy leaks:

- Chronic sleep deficit
- Unresolved open commitments (Zeigarnik effect — open loops consume background attention)
- Meaningless or repetitive meetings
- Constant notifications disrupting focus
- Conflicted or draining relationships (those consuming more than they give)
- Work that should belong to someone else
- Goals disconnected from mission

## 3. Design Rules for the Plan Tab

> **Rule: Track the base action, not just the outcome**  
> **Principle:** For any compound-type goal (skill, habit, health, financial), the app should track the *daily/weekly base action* — the thing that compounds — not just progress toward the final outcome.  
> **Rationale:** Compound outcomes lag the input by design. If users only see "no progress toward goal", they quit in the flat phase. Celebrating the input (kept the daily practice going) sustains behavior through the invisible zone. James Clear: "Every action is a vote for the type of person you are becoming."

> **Rule: Surface the invisible phase**  
> **Principle:** For habits and compound goals, show users explicitly which phase they are in (flat / slope / steep) based on their consistency duration. Frame the flat phase as normal and expected.  
> **Rationale:** Most goal abandonment happens because users don't know that "no visible results yet" is the designed experience for months 1–3. Making the phase visible removes the false signal that "this isn't working."

> **Rule: Energy labels on tasks, not just time**  
> **Principle:** When users add tasks to their plan board, prompt for an energy type (H/M/L) and energy type (physical / emotional / intellectual / spiritual). Surface warnings when the day's planned blocks exceed reasonable energy capacity.  
> **Rationale:** Two hours are not equal. A day of back-to-back H-blocks without recovery will produce poor quality output on all of them. The energy label is the minimum viable version of Margulan's power management system applied at the task level.

> **Rule: Recovery is a first-class planning citizen**  
> **Principle:** Recovery blocks should appear in the Plan tab and be treated with the same permanence as work commitments. They are not optional; they are not "free time that might become work".  
> **Rationale:** Margulan's core rule: recovery is a *prerequisite* for performance, not a reward after collapse. SDT research confirms that sustainable motivation requires psychological need satisfaction — rest satisfies the body/mind that makes the next period of motivation possible.

> **Rule: The energy audit surfaces in monthly review**  
> **Principle:** Monthly review should include: "Which activities raised your energy this month? Which drained it most? What should change?"  
> **Rationale:** Without periodic audit, users repeat draining patterns indefinitely. The personal energy map is built from repeated observation, not from a single assessment. The monthly review is where this learning is institutionalized.

> **Rule: Compound goals need a "hold the line" mode**  
> **Principle:** When a user has a compound-type goal (daily practice, building a skill), the app should have a minimal "hold the line" interaction — a single tap confirming the base action was done — even during difficult periods (illness, travel, high stress).  
> **Rationale:** The compound effect requires consistency. The minimum viable version of the habit is better than a break. Fogg's Tiny Habits: during hard times, shrink the behavior to its minimum but keep the chain. Margulan's energy management: the "Rest" mode should still include the minimum baseline ritual.

> **Rule: No hustle glorification**  
> **Principle:** No app copy, achievement label, or interaction should celebrate working at the expense of recovery. High completion + declining energy is a warning signal, not a badge of honor.  
> **Rationale:** Burnout research (Maslach): exhaustion is the first dimension of burnout, and it precedes depersonalization and reduced efficacy. An app that rewards working through exhaustion is accelerating burnout.

## 4. Psychology & Burnout Layer

### Compound interest of behavior (James Clear, Atomic Habits)
A 1% daily improvement compounds to 37x improvement over a year. A 1% daily degradation leaves 0.03 of original capacity. The math is not motivational — it is the behavioral description of kaizen. Small consistent inputs with no dramatic gestures.

**App implication:** Celebrate tiny consistent inputs with calm acknowledgment, not fanfare. The celebration should feel proportionate — a brief "done" — not a dopamine spike that makes the *celebration* more motivating than the *practice*.

### BJ Fogg — Motivation is unreliable
Fogg's central finding: designing for consistent motivation is a mistake. Motivation fluctuates enormously. Reliable behavior change requires making the target behavior *easy*, placing it in a clear context, and celebrating immediately. The compound effect requires surviving low-motivation days. That requires tiny habits (minimum viable versions), not waiting for motivation to return.

### Zeigarnik effect — open loops drain energy
Bluma Zeigarnik's finding: unfinished tasks occupy working memory until completed or explicitly closed. Every uncaptured commitment, every unresolved decision, every untouched item on a to-do list is a low-level background process consuming cognitive resources. This is why Margulan's capture-everything principle is an energy management tool, not just an organization tool: emptying the mind of open loops restores available attention.

**App implication:** The inbox-capture and weekly-review ritual is as much an energy tool as a planning tool. Surface this to users: "Your inbox has 14 uncaptured items. Processing them will clear your mental load."

### Ultradian rhythms (Peretz Lavie, Nathaniel Kleitman)
The human brain operates in 90-minute focus cycles (ultradian rhythms) followed by a 20-minute rest cycle. Attempting to maintain high focus for longer than 90 minutes without a break produces diminishing returns and accelerated fatigue. Margulan's 60–120 minute deep work blocks followed by real recovery are close to the scientifically observed natural human cycle.

**App implication:** Default deep work cluster length suggestion: 90 minutes. Default recovery between clusters: 20 minutes. Surface this as a starting point, adjustable by the user's observed personal pattern.

### The invisible phase and the "valley of disappointment" (James Clear)
Clear describes the invisible phase as a "valley of disappointment" — users expect linear progress, experience near-zero early results, and conclude the effort doesn't work. The compound curve is not linear until it suddenly is. Understanding this cognitively prevents the false signal from causing abandonment.

**App implication:** When a user is in the flat phase of a compound goal (weeks 1–8), surface the compound curve visualization occasionally. Not as a lecture — as a brief orientation: "You're in the early phase. This is normal. The curve is building."

### Burnout: energy depletion as a systems failure

Maslach's burnout model identifies six organizational mismatches that cause burnout:
1. Workload (too much)
2. Control (too little)
3. Reward (insufficient)
4. Community (disconnected)
5. Fairness (perceived injustice)
6. Values (conflict)

The energy management framework addresses #1 directly (WIP limits, energy budget), and #6 (mission alignment prevents values conflict). Margulan's planning system addresses #2 (the board gives control visibility) and #3 (completions are celebrated).

**Design implication:** An app that tracks only productivity metrics misses the burnout signals. The app should also track energy trend alongside completion trend. A divergence (high completions + falling energy) should trigger a gentle check-in, not a congratulation.

### Ancient wisdom parallels

| Tradition | Parallel |
|---|---|
| **Taoism — wu wei** | Effortless action through alignment, not force. The compound effect is wu wei: consistent small actions aligned with natural processes (growth, skill acquisition, relationship building) rather than forced bursts of heroic effort. |
| **Buddhist impermanence and patience** | The invisible phase requires accepting that nothing visible has yet arisen — and continuing anyway. This is the practice of the flat zone: acting without attachment to immediate result. |
| **Stoic discipline (askesis)** | Epictetus trained himself through consistent, repeated small practices — not grand transformations. Stoic discipline is daily, ordinary, and unglamorous. It is Kaizen without the Japanese name. |
| **Aristotle on eudaimonia (flourishing)** | Flourishing is not a moment — it is the ongoing activity of a life well-directed. The compound effect in a life is Aristotelian flourishing: built daily, never finished, always improving. |
| **Islamic concept of istiqama (steadfastness)** | Consistent, regular good action — even if small — is more beloved than occasional bursts. The Prophet Muhammad: "The most beloved of deeds to Allah are the most regular, even if small." This is the compound effect as a spiritual principle. |

## 5. Anti-patterns to Avoid

| Warning from transcript | App anti-pattern to avoid |
|---|---|
| "We live surrounded by stories of instant success — we see results but not the process" | Don't show before/after transformations or "in X days" messaging. This feeds the instant-success fantasy that makes the compound invisible phase unbearable. |
| "You start, then quickly abandon because it seems it's not working" | Don't only show outcome progress. Always show input consistency too. Outcome might be flat while input is strong — that's success in the flat zone. |
| "Recovery is not a reward after collapse — it is a condition of performance" | Never position rest as something you "earn" after completing a big task list. Recovery is structural, like sleep. |
| "Two free hours are not equal if one contains clarity and the other exhaustion" | Don't display available time without any energy context. A free hour in the evening after an exhausting day should not look the same as a fresh morning hour. |
| "Stop the leaks before adding replenishment" | Don't offer recovery practices to users without first helping them identify major energy drains. Adding morning rituals to a chronically sleep-deprived, commitment-overloaded life is cosmetic. |

### Psychological anti-patterns specific to compound goals
- **Early quitting signals** unrecognized as normal: The app must normalize the invisible phase actively. Silence about it causes abandonment.
- **Streak anxiety:** Tracking "X day streak" can flip from motivating to shaming when the streak breaks. A broken streak should never punish. Show instead: "You've done this 47 times. That's 47 votes for who you're becoming."
- **Recovery theater:** Users who schedule recovery but actually use it for low-grade scrolling or pseudo-work get the label without the benefit. Recovery prompts should invite genuine mode-switching.

## 6. Voice & Tone Notes

### For compound goals

**When a user has been consistent but sees no outcome yet:**
- ✓ "You've shown up 23 times. That's the work. The results are building."
- ✗ "No progress toward goal yet"

**When a compound goal is abandoned:**
- ✓ "Pausing this. The 23 sessions you did are still part of who you are — they don't disappear."
- ✓ Return message after pause: "Ready to pick this up? Nothing is reset."

**When showing the compound curve:**
- ✓ "Most of the results arrive after most of the time. You're in the early phase — this is exactly where it's supposed to look flat."

### For energy management

**When energy labels on a day are all H blocks:**
- ✓ "Full day of demanding work planned. Where's the recovery?"
- ✗ "Great productivity day ahead!"

**When a user adds a task beyond energy budget:**
- ✓ "Your energy looks fully planned for today. Move this to tomorrow, or decide what comes out?"
- ✗ "Task added" (silent)

**When battery drops below 40% during a check-in:**
- ✓ "Energy's low. What would help most right now?" (offer recovery menu)
- ✗ Any reference to tasks or goals

**After a period of sustained high completion:**
- ✓ "You've been working hard. How are you feeling?" (check energy, not celebrate workload)
- ✗ "Productivity streak!" 

### Margulan's tone on compound and energy

He speaks about these with genuine wonder — he finds the compound effect and energy rhythms genuinely fascinating, not as productivity hacks but as natural laws:
> *"This is one of the most powerful tools in the universe. And most people don't understand it."*

The app's tone should carry that sense of discovery. Not "here's a productivity tip." More: *"here's how reality actually works — and once you see it, you can't unsee it."*
